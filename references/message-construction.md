# Message construction

This skill produces two kinds of message: a **sponsor approach** and an **attendee invitation**. Both are drafts. The community's existing automation sends them, and a person approves every one.

An invitation arrives uninvited, in a stack of other mail, and gets a second of attention before someone decides to read or delete. Write for that second first and the body second.

## Layering

Three levels, and this file only carries the third:

1. **Word level** — kill the AI tells, prefer plain English, front-load the point, concrete over abstract, cut hedges like "just", "simply", "very". Match the community's voice rather than imposing a default.
2. **Email level** — voice, subject and preview mechanics, format limits.
3. **This file** — the decisions specific to inviting someone to an event on the strength of evidence the skill gathered.

Do not restate levels one and two here. They are true of every message the community sends.

## Decide the message's job first

Three questions before a word is written. If the event brief does not settle them, ask rather than assuming.

1. **What is the one action?** RSVP, reply, book a sponsor call, forward to a colleague. One. A message asking for three things has not decided what it is for, and it hands that work to the reader in the seconds they gave you.
2. **What does this person already know?** A prior attendee and a cold investor need different first lines, and one message cannot serve both. The queue already knows which they are — use it.
3. **What happens if they do nothing?** If the honest answer is nothing, this may not need to be sent, and saying so is more useful than sending it.

## Shape

Event outreach is almost always **the whole thing**, not a pointer: the invitation is the deliverable, and there is no long piece to click through to. It can still be short. Four moves:

1. **The reason this arrived now**, in the first line — the dated signal the queue selected.
2. **What the event is**, in one line: what kind of room, when, where.
3. **Why this person specifically** — the fit, stated plainly, one sentence.
4. **The one action.**

The failure shape is the accidental third form: half a sponsorship deck pasted above a "let me know if you're interested". It spends the attention on material the reader did not ask for and still asks them to do the work of deciding.

## What to cut

Whole categories, not proportional trimming.

- The community's origin story and the history of the event series. The reader's reason to care goes where the sender's story wants to be.
- Anything hedged at length. Nuance survives on a page and dies in a preview pane.
- Supporting reasons after the first. The queue selected one reason precisely so the message would not need three.
- Sponsorship tier tables, capacity math, and logistics. Those belong on the page or in the reply.
- Secondary calls to action. Move them to the reply or drop them.

What survives: the specific dated signal, the event in one line, the fit in one sentence, the action.

## What to add

- **A reason this arrived now.** Unexplained mail reads as bulk. This is the queue's `reason_to_engage`, said in the reader's language.
- **Why they are on this list**, when the relationship is thin or old.
- **Subject and preview text as a pair**, written after the body, when you know what the message actually says. Front-load the real content; the preview extends the subject rather than repeating it, and it is never left unset — the client will otherwise fill it with whatever the HTML starts with.
- **A plain text version.**
- **A sign-off from a person**, not from the community as an entity.

## Keep the evidence honest

This is the part specific to this skill, and the part most likely to go wrong.

The message is built on a claim the skill gathered, with a source and a date. Compressing that claim into a subject line is exactly where it gets hardened.

- **Do not strengthen a hedged signal while shortening it.** "Announced a first close" becoming "just raised their fund" is the easiest error in this pass and the one that costs a relationship.
- **Do not invent specifics.** A number, a date, or a mutual connection that was not in the evidence does not go in the message.
- **Relationship language is precise or absent.** Co-attendance is co-attendance. It is not "great to reconnect", and it is never an introduction unless someone agreed to make one.
- **No manufactured personalization.** A merge field wrapped in "I saw you're doing great work" is worse than no personalization, because it tells the reader exactly how the message was made.
- **No fake urgency or scarcity.** Capacity is real in this workflow — say the real number or say nothing. Invented scarcity trains the list to distrust the sender, and it costs more than the RSVP it buys.
- **Specific beats clever.** A subject that says what is inside keeps its promise, and keeping the promise is what protects the next send to that person.

When a draft drops a caveat or changes the emphasis of the evidence, say so in the review packet. The reviewer should get the chance to disagree.

## Per-message review

Review is three-state on the message as well as the candidate:

- **approve** — the message goes to the queue as written
- **hold** — right person, wrong message; the message is revised, the candidate keeps their place
- **reject** — the claim does not hold up, which is a signal about the evidence and not only about the copy

A message rejected for overstating its evidence should send you back to the queue row, not to the thesaurus.

## Shorter is always better

The community's stated preference, in its own words: *"Shorter is always better haha!"* Treat it as a house rule rather than a suggestion.

**Shortening sponsor copy is a recurring task, not a one-off.** Sponsors send a paragraph written for their own site; the event page and the invitation need two sentences. Cut to what the audience needs to know about them, keep the claim at its original strength, and hand back what you dropped.

A worked example from the community's own thread, after several rounds:

> Stifel is a diversified global wealth management, investment bank, and commercial banking firm. Stifel Venture Banking provides commercial banking and debt capital financing solutions to venture capital-backed technology companies and their investors.

Two sentences: who they are, and what they do for this specific audience. No history, no adjectives, no claims the sponsor did not make.

## Patterns

**Sponsor approach.** Network scale first, then the calendar, then the one dated reason this sponsor fits, then the action. A sponsor is buying access to the audience, so audience scale *is* their reason to care — leading with it is correct here and only here. The community's real pitch opens exactly this way: *"Between VC Village and MATCH HOUSE we are at well over 1000+ investor members/attendees and even more founders."*

Present the calendar and price plainly. It fails when it opens with tiers and payment mechanics — that is the second conversation, and putting it first turns an invitation into an invoice. It also fails when it asks late: sponsors want roughly two months of lead time, so a queue that surfaces a sponsor three weeks out has already lost the deal it is recommending.

**Sponsor qualification reply.** When a sponsor asks who is actually in the room, answer with practical ranges and named examples, not adjectives. The working answer looks like *"founders range from under $100k ARR through $2m ARR plus"* and *"GP/LP dinners have a lot of emerging managers"*, plus a prior event and the returning/new split. Vague audience answers are what stall sponsor threads.

**Attendee invitation, prior attendee.** Lead with the return: name the event they came to. It fails when it treats a returning guest as a cold contact, which reads as a community that does not know its own room.

**Attendee invitation, new.** Lead with the dated signal and why this room is relevant to it. It fails when it opens with the community's credentials instead of the reader's reason.

**Follow-up.** One follow-up, tied to something new — capacity closing for real, a speaker confirmed, a peer attending who agreed to be named. A follow-up with nothing new is a second ask, and the decline cooldown exists so it does not become a third.

**A reply ends the sequence.** Any reply, bounce, opt-out, or rejection stops further automated outreach to that person for this event. A human takes it from there. Sending a scheduled follow-up to someone who already answered is the fastest way to make an automated system obvious.

## Grounding the voice

The community supplied one real sponsor thread and no library of approved investor or attendee messages. Until such a library exists, sponsor copy follows the worked example above and attendee copy follows the rules in this file — and the review gate is doing more work than it should. **An approved message library is a named missing input**, not an optional nicety: it is what turns "concise and specific" from an instruction into a standard.
